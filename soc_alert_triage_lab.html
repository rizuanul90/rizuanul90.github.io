SOC // Alert Triage

SOC L1 Alert Triage

A hands-on lab in reviewing, prioritizing, and resolving security alerts the way a first-line SOC analyst does — working through a live alert queue, deciding what to investigate first, and closing each case with a documented verdict.

✓ Complete TryHackMe — Premium Room SOC Dashboard / SIEM Alert Triage ~60 min

01Overview

This lab simulates a real SOC alert queue: a dashboard full of alerts moving between New, In Progress, and Closed states, each with its own severity, status, and description. The goal isn't to clear the queue — it's to build a repeatable process for deciding what to look at first, how to investigate it properly, and how to close it with a defensible verdict.

I went in treating this less like a checklist and more like the job itself: every alert is either noise that's wasting analyst time, or the first sign of an attacker who's already inside. The job of an L1 analyst is to tell the difference quickly and correctly, every single time.

02Triage Approach

Before touching any individual alert, I worked through the lifecycle every alert moves through on its way from raw log to closed case:

  1. Event generation — a login, a process launch, a file download, something happens on an endpoint, network device, or cloud service.
  2. Log shipping — that event gets forwarded into a centralized SIEM or EDR platform.
  3. Detection logic fires — a correlation rule matches the event and an alert is generated, with a name, severity, and description attached.
  4. Triage — an analyst (me, in this case) picks up the alert, investigates it, and assigns a verdict.
  5. Resolution — the alert is closed as a true positive (real threat, escalate) or false positive (benign, document and move on).

The reason this matters: a SOC doesn't fail because nobody saw the alert. It fails because the alert sat in the queue too long, or got triaged by someone who didn't understand what it was actually telling them.

03Prioritization — Choosing What to Work First

With dozens of alerts sitting in the queue, the first real decision isn't "how do I investigate this," it's "which one do I investigate first." I used a three-pass filter to pick:

  • Filter out anything already owned. If another analyst already assigned themselves or it's mid-investigation, it's not mine to take.
  • Sort by severity first. Critical and high-severity alerts get worked before medium or low — detection rules are tuned so the highest severities carry the highest real-world risk.
  • Sort by age second. Between two alerts of equal severity, the older one wins. An attacker who breached a host an hour ago has had an hour longer to move laterally, exfiltrate data, or establish persistence than one who just tripped a rule a minute ago.
Defender's note

This is also why a SOC's mean-time-to-triage (MTTT) metric matters more than raw alert volume. A queue with 200 low-priority alerts and one missed critical alert is a worse outcome than a queue with 50 alerts that all got worked in the right order.

04Working the Queue

Three representative alerts from the queue, each handled through the same investigate → decide → close cycle. Exact dashboard data, screenshots, and submission flags are intentionally omitted here in favor of the actual analytical reasoning — that's the part that's reusable on the job and the part a portfolio should actually show.

Alert 1 — Unusual VPN Login Location

Unusual VPN Login Location High
Affected UserService account, finance dept.
SourceVPN gateway logs
TriggerLogin from geolocation inconsistent with user's normal pattern
StatusNew → In Progress

Investigation: I checked whether the account had any recent travel notice or VPN exception on file (none), then looked at the login's surrounding context: time of day relative to the user's normal working hours, whether MFA was satisfied, and whether the same session showed any unusual follow-on activity (file access, privilege use). A login from an unexpected location on a service account — which shouldn't be logging in interactively from anywhere — is a much stronger signal than the same anomaly on a human user who travels.

Reasoning: Service accounts are high-value targets precisely because nobody expects to see "unusual" behavior questioned as often as a human's — they're often over-permissioned and under-monitored. Treating the alert with extra weight because of who triggered it, not just what triggered it, is part of accurate triage.

Verdict: True Positive Escalated to L2 for credential rotation and session review

Alert 2 — Unapproved Mimikatz Usage

Unapproved Mimikatz Usage Critical
Affected HostDomain-joined workstation
SourceEDR process telemetry
TriggerKnown credential-dumping tool signature detected in process tree
StatusNew → In Progress

Investigation: Mimikatz has almost no legitimate enterprise use case outside of authorized red team engagements, so the first thing I checked was whether this host was in scope for any scheduled penetration test or purple team exercise. It wasn't. From there I looked at the parent process that spawned it, the logged-on user context, and whether the process actually executed or was blocked/quarantined by the EDR before it could touch LSASS memory.

Reasoning: Severity and verdict aren't the same decision. This one was correctly flagged Critical by the detection rule regardless of outcome — the tool itself is the risk signal. But whether it's a true positive still depends on execution context: a blocked, quarantined Mimikatz binary that never ran is a very different outcome than one that successfully dumped credentials and now has a live attacker with valid domain creds.

Verdict: True Positive Escalated immediately — credential compromise scope assessment

Alert 3 — Email Marked as Phishing

Email Marked as Phishing Medium
Affected UserGeneral staff mailbox
SourceUser-reported / mail gateway flag
TriggerInbound email reported by recipient, gateway confidence: medium
StatusNew → In Progress

Investigation: This is the highest-volume, lowest-signal alert type most SOCs deal with, so the workflow has to be fast without being careless. I checked the sender domain against known-good corporate domains, looked at whether the email contained a link or attachment, and — if a link was present — whether it pointed to a credential-harvesting page or a benign marketing redirect. I also checked whether other users had received the same message, which would indicate a broader campaign rather than a one-off.

Reasoning: Most alerts in this category resolve as false positives — marketing emails, internal newsletters a user didn't recognize, legitimate vendor correspondence with an unusual sender display name. The discipline here isn't suspicion, it's consistency: every alert gets the same minimum verification regardless of how "obviously fine" it looks, because that's exactly the category attackers try to blend into.

Verdict: False Positive Documented and closed — legitimate vendor email, flagged on sender mismatch

05Why This Matters (Defender Perspective)

It would be easy to read this lab as "click through some alerts and get the right answer." The actual skill being built is judgment under volume and ambiguity — the same judgment that determines whether a real breach gets caught in minutes or sits unnoticed for weeks.

Failure ModeReal-World Consequence
Wrong prioritizationA critical alert sits behind ten low-severity ones; attacker dwell time increases
Shallow investigationA true positive gets closed as false positive on a surface-level look — the most dangerous outcome in this lab
Alert fatigueHigh false-positive volume on rules like phishing reports causes analysts to rubber-stamp closures
No documentationVerdict without reasoning means L2 can't trust or audit the L1 decision, and patterns across alerts go unnoticed

06Key Takeaways

  • Severity is set by the detection rule; verdict is set by the analyst — the two are independent decisions and conflating them leads to either over-escalation or missed threats.
  • Who or what triggered the alert changes how much scrutiny it deserves, even when the technical signature looks identical (a service account vs. a human user, for example).
  • Prioritization by severity-then-age isn't just a process rule — it directly maps to limiting attacker dwell time, which is one of the metrics that matters most to a SOC's actual effectiveness.
  • The highest-volume alert categories (phishing reports, login anomalies) are also where consistency matters most, because that's where fatigue-driven shortcuts do the most damage.
  • A closed alert without a documented reasoning trail is functionally unverifiable — writing the "why" is part of the job, not an extra step.
